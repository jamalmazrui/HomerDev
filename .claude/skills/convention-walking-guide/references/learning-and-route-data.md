# Learning Routes and Maintaining Their Data

Read when source documents offer travel-learning methods, venue discovery, transport handoffs, or crowdsourced updates. Use the methods without importing another venue's route facts.

## Support self-directed learning

Place general discovery and travel-learning advice in an optional appendix, keeping the main guide focused on the venue and event. If useful, offer a short sequence: room/elevator and essential access first; registration and immediate convention destinations next; optional amenities and local exploration later. Let the traveler choose directions, a demonstration, a human guide, or independent exploration. Do not assign universal practice times, require repeated unassisted walks, or imply accepting help reduces independence.

For each supported route ask:

- What fixed feature confirms progress?
- What indicates overshooting?
- Where can the traveler regain orientation?
- How does the return journey differ?
- What changes after closing time or when the event layout changes?

Do not infer answers from a label. Sound, smell, crowds, music, and movable furniture can support a traveler's personal map when observed, but are less dependable than fixed route geometry. Never invent a coffee grinder, carpet edge, or audible elevator from a floor plan. Do not prescribe cane techniques or dog-handling commands from generic directory descriptions. Offer appropriate refresher resources separately if useful; verify the source, intended audience, content, and accessible format. A directory listing is a research lead, not proof that a lesson was watched or that its advice is right for this traveler.

## Treat the trip as connected segments

Connect station/airport, boarding point, vehicle drop-off, public pedestrian path, actual building entrance, lobby/elevator, and session entrance. Confirm each handoff. Do not substitute a postal address or approximate GPS pin for an accessible public door. Distinguish arrival and departure stops when they differ. Document a fallback for a missed shuttle, lift closure, or locked door when available; keep a fallback unknown when it has not been checked.

Maintain three distinct information classes in the worksheet:

1. Provider-published static facts: plans, entrances, normal service schedules.
2. Provider-issued current changes: room switches, outages, closures, revised pickup arrangements.
3. Traveler observations: useful reports that need attribution, time, context, and validation.

Keep these separate from the route's Published/Walked/From map/Ask on site tag. A walkthrough describes past verification, not a guarantee against later closures. Source age and route status are independent.

## Add structured fields where useful

Use lists, not a dense table, unless requested. Include only fields that help checking or maintaining the route:

- Route ID and title.
- Exact origin/destination: property, building, floor, door, and street side if relevant.
- Starting position/facing direction.
- Actions, decision points, confirmation cue, overshoot cue, recovery point.
- Fixed versus temporary landmarks.
- Level changes, accessible alternative, and reverse/reentry differences.
- Operating hours, event configuration, and after-hours restrictions.
- Source class, source date, checker/date, and conditions last confirmed.
- Current correction contact, change reason, and next review.
- Primary and fallback handoff for transport connections.

Do not manufacture data to fill a schema. Avoid publishing private guest-room numbers, live locations, or checker contact information without authorization. Offer an accessible correction process using an already published organizer contact; record route ID, place, travel direction, time, and observed change. Mark reports as observations until validated. Do not send reports or contact anyone unless authorized.

## Verify technology identity and venue coverage

Follow links and inspect the actual destination title, publisher, app ID, and content. A successful HTTP response can be an unrelated or repurposed domain. A service's historical existence or an indexed old brochure does not establish its current availability. Withhold unrelated redirects and look for current provider or venue-hosted alternatives.

For indoor systems, check the exact property and coverage: entrances, floors, current routes, localization requirements, app version, phone hardware, network, and whether tactile maps or special markers actually exist. Unconfirmed coverage is unknown, not evidence of absence. Ordinary QR codes, NaviLens codes, beacons, camera localization, and outdoor GPS are different systems; do not imply one substitutes for another.

Match reviews to product lineage and date. A store can retain reviews from a previous paid product after a rename. Label those as historical experience and verify current free functionality/platforms independently. A developer interview is not an independent hands-on review. Do not infer Android performance from an iPhone review. Keep paid services and time-limited free offers separate from permanently free app functions.

## Optional nearby outings

Use an optional outing record when nearby meals, errands, or attractions help attendees. Start from a named hotel door and end at the actual visitor entrance. Record pedestrian conditions, evidence for distance, rest options, complete travel/wait/activity/return time, session fit, last return transport, and an alternative. Do not equate a short map distance with an easy walk or impose one traveler's comfortable walking range on all attendees.

Name the actual nonvisual access: sound/touch/taste experience, ordinary guide narration, arranged verbal description, formal audio description, braille/large-print information, or permitted touch exploration. Distinguish natural listening value from verified interpretive access and wheelchair access from nonvisual access. Identify source/date, advance-notice period, contact, permission/reservation, cost, and meeting/pickup point where needed. Do not imply an unarranged accommodation is booked. Reuse one full entry with cross-links for recurring options rather than duplicating changing details.

## Bickford: landmarks and individual preferences

Thomas Bickford’s *The Care and Feeding of the Long White Cane* (NFB, 1993), especially Environmental Clues and Mental Maps, supports giving a usable spatial overview and respecting differences in preferred directions. Some travelers favor mental maps and compass bearings; others prefer left/right sequences. Give only bearings established by the evidence. Treat environmental sounds and time/distance estimates as potentially useful personal observations, not universal landmarks. Put general recovery advice and learning suggestions in the optional appendix. Do not convert historical cane technique, traffic advice, or legal statements into current recommendations. [Official book contents](https://nfb.org/sites/default/files/images/nfb/publications/books/cfcane/canetc.htm).

## Additional map formats and collection

Search embedded meeting-space viewers as well as PDF links. Public Cvent viewer HTML may expose `window.__main` bootstrap JSON with `propertyConfig`, room labels, and `floorplanimage`/`floorIMG` URLs. Preserve source URL, property identity, room label, and retrieval date alongside the PNG. A field named floor or campus map can still contain only a single room outline: inspect it visually before using it as a connected plan. Seek SVG, PNG/JPEG, brochures, evacuation drawings, and original office documents as appropriate. If local retrieval would help, provide a dependency-free collector with a source manifest, download limits, readable logging, and a results ZIP; do not claim it executes interactive viewers.
